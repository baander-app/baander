# Register: Steve Jobs (product introductions)

Jobs introduced a product by starting where the audience already was: a problem they
had, an object in their pocket, a habit they recognized. He said what the product was
in one sentence, turned each specification into something a listener could picture,
and showed it working one action at a time. The excitement came from the facts he chose
and the order he put them in. Sources read for this sheet, from the recordings'
auto-generated English captions: the iPod introduction of 23 October 2001
(<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=utlT57WK8a8>), the Macworld San Francisco keynote of
9 January 2007 that introduced iPhone (<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7l-Pa0uCod0>),
and the Macworld San Francisco keynote of 15 January 2008 that introduced MacBook Air
(<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=i9XU6wk_mWY>). Each quote keeps the caption's words
and gives its timestamp in that recording; punctuation and capitals are added where the
captions have none.

**Assign Jobs to:** the opening of a website page that presents a feature, as the house
style maps it, and any text whose request names this register. Never choose it by
purpose alone. The body of a page keeps the register the house style gives it.

A keynote is spoken, staged and timed. This register keeps the techniques that survive on
a page and leaves the stage devices behind. The claim rules in `../SKILL.md` ("Present a
feature") govern every sentence it writes: each claim needs a source, planned features
read as planned, and limits stay on the page.

## The principles, with the source in evidence

### 1. Start from the reader's moment, not the product

The first sentence is a situation the reader has been in. The product arrives as the
answer to it.

> "How many times have you gone on the road with a CD player" (iPod, 00:15:14)

> "The problem is that they're not so smart and they're not so easy to use." (iPhone,
> 00:30:10, on the smartphones of the day)

Neither line mentions the new product. The reader recognizes the problem first and is
already waiting for a way out when the product appears.

### 2. Say what it is in one sentence

> "What is the MacBook Air in a sentence? It's the world's thinnest notebook."
> (MacBook Air, 00:55:02)

One sentence that a reader could repeat to someone else. For Bånder that sentence says
what the reader gets, not where the product ranks: a ranking needs a measurement and a
comparison, and an opening has room for neither.

### 3. Turn a specification into something the reader can hold

A number on its own is a spec sheet. Jobs restated each one as a thing the audience
already knew.

> "That hard drive is 5 gigabytes in capacity, 5 gigabytes which holds a thousand
> songs" (iPod, 00:16:13)

> "iPod is the size of a deck of cards" (iPod, 00:19:57)

> "It's so thin it even fits inside one of these envelopes that we've all seen floating
> around the office" (MacBook Air, 00:57:37)

The thousand songs are arithmetic, not decoration. He named the bit rate seconds later
(00:16:24): 160 kbit/s for a four-minute song is about 4.8 MB, and 5 GB holds about
1,040 of those. A translated figure in Bånder copy has the same footing: a cited source
fact, a stated assumption such as the song length, and the arithmetic, all recorded in
the claim ledger.

### 4. Explain the mechanism in words the reader already owns

> "We're going to use a pointing device that we're all born with. We're born with 10 of
> them. We're going to use our fingers." (iPhone, 00:33:19)

> "Every application wants a slightly different user interface, a slightly optimized set
> of buttons just for it. And what happens if you think of a great idea six months from
> now? You can't run around and add a button to these things. They're already shipped."
> (iPhone, 00:31:54)

The second passage explains why fixed buttons fail before it offers a remedy. The reader
follows the cause in the order it happens and arrives at the design without being told
it is clever.

### 5. Show it working, one action at a time

> "Well, how do I scroll through my list of artists? How do I do this? I just take my
> finger and I scroll. That's it." (iPhone, 00:42:26)

> "When I don't need the keyboard, it's not there. When I do, it's there." (iPhone,
> 01:06:30)

A page cannot run a demo, but it can describe one action and its result, and the page's
screenshot can show the rest. Keep the copy consistent with the screenshot beside it.

### 6. Name the limit plainly, then say what the reader does instead

> "One thing you're not going to find in a MacBook Air is an optical drive"
> (MacBook Air, 01:06:46)

> "What do we normally do with optical drives? We play movies, right? We install
> software, we make backups and we burn music CDs" (MacBook Air, 01:07:19)

He stated the missing part without softening it, then walked through what people used
the part for and how each need was met. Bånder copy states its limits the same way: the
qualifier that a claim needs stays beside the claim, and the other limits move to a
labeled section on the same page rather than leaving it.

## What stays on the stage

These devices work in a hall with a live audience. Written down, they read as filler,
and several make claims the copy cannot support.

| Device | In the recordings | Write instead |
|---|---|---|
| Superlatives and enthusiasm adjectives | "Multi-touch, which is phenomenal. It works like magic." (iPhone, 00:33:30); "this is a quantum leap" (iPod, 00:15:06) | The fact that prompted the word |
| The reveal and its teasing question | "Are you getting it?" (iPhone, 00:29:00), after three products turn out to be one | The most important fact first |
| Triads for rhythm | The iPhone's three products, repeated before the reveal | The items that are true; two is a fine number |
| "It's not X, it's Y" reversals | | The claim, stated once |
| Rows of fragments | | Sentences; a two-line headline is the exception |
| Named rivals | Four rival phones on screen (iPhone, 00:31:25); "this is that Sony product again" (MacBook Air, 00:56:53) | What the reader does today, or a measurement on a page about measurements |
| Promises about later | | Planned features labeled as planned |
| Props, applause pauses, live demos | The envelope (MacBook Air, 00:58:03) | The page's screenshot and one described action |

The iPod comparison with "a USB player" (00:17:27) sits between the last two rows: it
compares against a kind of product the audience owned, not a named rival. Bånder copy
uses the reader's present routine instead, sparingly.

## Before / after (Bånder documentation → Jobs register)

These examples show the technique on documented Bånder behaviour. Every "after" claim
cites its source, as a claim ledger would; verify the target page's facts rather than
treating these examples as a current specification.

**Before** (`README.md`, "Listen in the web player"): "The equalizer offers presets and
graphic or parametric controls, with saved profiles for different audio devices.
Additional controls include compression, stereo width and crossfeed, allowing listeners
to adjust the sound for their headphones or speakers."
**After:** "Headphones and speakers don't sound alike, so Bånder lets you keep a sound
for each. Start from a preset or shape it with graphic or parametric EQ, add
compression, stereo width or crossfeed, and save it as that device's profile. Switch
devices, switch profiles."
**Ledger:** "Headphones and speakers don't sound alike": framing, no product claim.
Presets, graphic and parametric EQ, compression, stereo width, crossfeed and
per-device profiles: `README.md`, "Listen in the web player". Activating a saved
profile: `docs-book/part-2-developer-guide/contexts/user-preference.md` (EQ device
profiles with activate and deactivate).

**Before** (`docs-book/part-2-developer-guide/contexts/session.md`): "The Session
context coordinates an account's playback destination, media selection, music queue,
and playback position, alongside registered user devices."
**After:** "Start an album on the laptop in the kitchen and finish it on the computer in
the living room, wherever you're signed in to Bånder. The song, the queue and the
position move with you, and so does
whether it was playing or paused. Casting to AirPlay, Chromecast and DLNA receivers is
planned and not available yet."
**Ledger:** selection, queue, position and playing state follow the transfer, and each
signed-in browser tab is a playback endpoint:
`docs-book/part-2-developer-guide/contexts/session.md` (the context summary and the
`ListeningSession` and `PlaybackTransfer` rows). Casting is planned: `ROADMAP.md`,
playback destinations, where receiver qualification remains open.

The homepage's own destinations headline, "Start in one room. Keep listening in
another.", already does what principle 1 asks; a rewrite keeps it.

## Jobs checklist

- [ ] The first sentence is a moment or problem the reader has had, not a product name
      or a boast.
- [ ] One sentence says what the feature does for the reader.
- [ ] Each specification the copy keeps is restated as something the reader can picture,
      and each translated figure has its arithmetic in the claim ledger.
- [ ] Mechanisms are explained in words the reader already owns, in the order they
      happen.
- [ ] The copy describes one action and its result, consistent with the page's
      screenshot.
- [ ] Every documented limit stays on the page: beside the claim when the claim is false
      without it, otherwise in a labeled section.
- [ ] Planned features read as planned.
- [ ] No superlatives, enthusiasm adjectives, reveals, reversals, padded triads or rows
      of fragments.
- [ ] No competing product is named.
- [ ] Every claim appears in the claim ledger with its source.
